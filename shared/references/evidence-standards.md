# Evidence Standards

## Source tiers

| Tier | Examples | How to use |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Primary / official | Government and regulator publications, central banks, official statistics, court rulings, university admissions pages, peer-reviewed papers, company filings | Strongest. Quote these when available. |
| 2. Reputable reporting | Major news organisations with editorial standards, reporting on tier 1 material | Good for events and context; check numbers against tier 1 when possible. |
| 3. Industry bodies | Trade associations, professional bodies | Useful; note that they advocate for their members. |
| 4. Vendor / consultancy | Security vendors, consultancies, marketing reports | Label as "according to <vendor>"; methodology often unclear; avoid as the only source for a number. |
| 5. Aggregators, blogs, forums, AI-generated pages | SEO sites, unknown blogs, social posts | Leads only. Do not cite as evidence. |

## Checks for every number

- Who measured it, and how?
- What period does it cover (year, quarter, "last three years")?
- Does a second independent source agree?
- Is it a forecast or a measurement?
- Has it been rounded or exaggerated in the retelling?

## Conflicting sources

1. Report the conflict.
2. Prefer the higher tier and the more recent source.
3. If still unclear, use only the part all sources agree on, and give the verdict **Not enough evidence** for the rest.

## Recency

- For policy, prices, rankings, statistics and news: check the publication date, and say how recent it is.
- For "current" claims, search with the current year in the query.

## Reasoning checks (non-factual claims)

- Correlation vs causation.
- Category mistakes (e.g. "fraud detection is CS, not data science": fields overlap; check how the target programs describe themselves).
- Survivorship and selection bias in examples.
- Whether the claim is about averages or a specific case.
