At 12:17 on a Tuesday afternoon, my program announced that every student in our peer tutoring club had been assigned a tutor. I was pretty proud of it. Then our club coordinator pointed out that three students had no one to meet, two tutors had been put in the same room, and one student had been matched with someone who wasn't even free that day.

I stared at the screen for a few seconds. The program had done exactly what I'd written. The trouble was what I'd written.

I had volunteered to build a small Python program to help organize our school's peer tutoring club. Until then, the coordinator had been running everything through spreadsheets, messages, and a remarkable memory for everyone's schedules. The fix seemed obvious to me: collect everyone's subjects and availability, compare them, and find the best matches.

It sounded like a job made for a computer.

For the first few days, I enjoyed it more than I expected. I liked breaking a messy task into smaller pieces. I liked watching a few lines of code turn into something useful. When the matching algorithm finally ran without errors, I sent the results to the coordinator almost right away.

That's when I found out how little I had thought about the people using it.

Some students had written their availability in ways my program couldn't read. Others had ticked every time slot because they didn't know which sessions would suit them. One student had a specific reason for asking for a particular tutor, but my form never gave them anywhere to say so.

I had squeezed everyone's situation into a few spreadsheet columns.

My first instinct was to fix it all by piling more conditions into the code. But after talking to the coordinator, I saw that the real problem was how I had collected the information. I had built the system around what was easy for me to program, not what was easy for the people using it.

So I started over. I changed the form, added an optional box for special requests, and had the program suggest matches instead of making the final call. The coordinator could look over the suggestions and talk to students before confirming anything.

It wasn't an impressive piece of software. No machine learning, no clever algorithm. If anything, the final version was less technically ambitious than my first try.

But it was more useful.

That changed how I think about computer science. I used to see programming mostly as figuring out the right instructions to give a machine. Now I think it starts well before the first line of code: deciding which problem is worth solving, asking the right questions, and understanding the people the solution affects.

I still enjoy spending an unreasonable amount of time debugging. There's something satisfying about tracking down the one small mistake behind a much bigger problem. But I've also learned that not every problem can be solved by staring at the code.

I want to study computer science because I like the mix of logic, creativity, and uncertainty that comes with it. I'm especially interested in how computing, data, and artificial intelligence can help people make better decisions. At Harvard, I hope to explore those areas and learn from people who come at problems differently than I do. I don't expect to show up with all the answers. Honestly, I'd rather be around people who make me realize how many questions I haven't thought to ask.

I still have the original version of that tutoring program on my laptop. Sometimes I open it up and look at the code. It reminds me how excited I was the first time it ran.

It also reminds me that a program can be technically correct and still get the problem completely wrong.
