At 12:17 on a Tuesday afternoon, my program announced that every student in our peer tutoring club had been assigned a tutor. I was pretty proud of it. Our club coordinator then pointed out that three students had no one to meet, two tutors had been assigned to the same room, and one student had been matched with someone who wasn't even available that day.

I stared at the screen for a few seconds. The program had worked exactly as I had written it. I had just written it badly.

I had volunteered to build a small Python program to help organize our school's peer tutoring club. Until then, the coordinator had run everything through spreadsheets and messages, plus a remarkable ability to remember everyone's schedules. I thought the fix was straightforward. Collect everyone's subjects and availability, compare them and find the best matches, which sounded like exactly the kind of problem a computer is made for.

For the first few days, I enjoyed the process more than I expected. I liked breaking a messy task into smaller pieces and watching a few lines of code turn into something useful. When I finally got the matching algorithm to run without errors, I sent the results to the coordinator almost immediately.

I hadn't thought much about the people who would be using it. Some students had written their availability in ways my program couldn't understand. Others had selected every available time because they didn't know which sessions would be comfortable for them. One student had a specific reason for requesting a particular tutor, but my form had never given them a place to explain it. I had reduced everyone's situation to a few columns in a spreadsheet.

My first instinct was to fix everything by adding more conditions to the code. After talking to the coordinator, though, I realized the bigger problem was the way I had collected the information. I had designed the system around what was convenient for me to program, not what was convenient for the people using it.

So I went back to the beginning. I changed the form and added an optional section for special requests. I also made the program suggest possible matches instead of making the final decision, so the coordinator could review the suggestions and speak with students before confirming anything.

The final version was not an especially impressive piece of software. It used no machine learning and no complicated algorithm, and it was probably less technically ambitious than my first attempt. It was also more useful.

Before this, I mostly saw programming as finding the right instructions to give a machine. I now think it begins long before the first line of code, with deciding which problem is worth solving, asking the right questions and understanding the people the solution will affect.

I still enjoy spending an unreasonable amount of time debugging a program, and I love finding the one small mistake behind a much bigger problem. I've also learned that not every problem can be solved by looking at the code.

I want to study computer science because I enjoy how it combines logic, creativity and uncertainty. I'm particularly interested in how computing and data, including artificial intelligence, can help people make better decisions. At Harvard, I hope to explore these areas while learning from people who approach problems differently from me. I don't expect to arrive with all the answers. Honestly, I'd rather be surrounded by people who make me realize how many questions I haven't thought to ask.

The original version of that tutoring program is still saved on my laptop. Every so often I open it and scroll through the code. It reminds me how excited I was when it first ran, and that a program can be technically correct and still get the problem completely wrong.
